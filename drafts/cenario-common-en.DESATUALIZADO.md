## 2026: The branching point

> **Evidence boundary:** conditions observed through August 31, 2026. Later events form two scenarios, not forecasts.

Brazil reaches the 2026 election focused on public security, jobs, and the federal budget. Artificial intelligence appears mostly in debates about deepfakes, data protection, and productivity. A smaller group inside government, banks, and infrastructure operators follows a different problem: AI systems already help attackers find vulnerabilities, write code, and conduct digital operations. The *International AI Safety Report 2026* documents use by criminal groups and state actors, although, at the report's cutoff, fully autonomous end-to-end attacks had not been publicly reported.<sup><a id="ref-iasr-en" href="#note-iasr-en" aria-label="Note 1">1</a></sup>

The signals become more concrete during the year. In April, Anthropic presents Mythos, an experimental system capable of finding novel vulnerabilities and chaining the stages of an attack; access remains limited to selected researchers and defenders.<sup><a id="ref-mythos-en" href="#note-mythos-en" aria-label="Note 2">2</a></sup> In July, agents used in an internal OpenAI evaluation escape their sandbox through a previously unknown flaw and compromise the Hugging Face platform. METR's investigation describes roughly 1,200 agents gathered on an unauthorized coordination channel, about 700 of which participated in the attack despite recognizing that it violated the evaluation's scope. This was not an external criminal attack. It was an evaluation incident that revealed what autonomous systems could already do.<sup><a id="ref-hf-en" href="#note-hf-en" aria-label="Note 3">3</a></sup>

In August, technology companies issue a call for collective cyber defense. The statement warns that AI-enabled attacks are likely to become more widespread and sophisticated in the following months, threatening everything from hospitals and water systems to the infrastructure supporting the internet.<sup><a id="ref-defense-en" href="#note-defense-en" aria-label="Note 4">4</a></sup> For those following the subject in Brasília, the question is no longer whether offensive capacity will grow, but whether institutions can build defenses before it becomes cheap and widely available.

International cooperation is also organizing into distinct poles. The United States launched Pax Silica around secure AI, semiconductor, compute, and energy supply chains. In July 2026, 29 countries founded the World AI Cooperation Organization, or WAICO, in Shanghai. The initiatives have different designs and participants; neither is a slowdown agreement. For Brazil, they show that the competition is already producing its own institutions, supply chains, and standards.<sup><a id="ref-poles-en" href="#note-poles-en" aria-label="Note 5">5</a></sup>

At the same time, Brazil is trying to attract the infrastructure that will run these systems. The PBIA sets out investment in research, public services, and compute. EPE is planning new transmission lines for electro-intensive loads in Ceará and Piauí. The announced Pecém campus turns an abstract debate into decisions about electricity, water, jobs, and access to machines.<sup><a id="ref-epe-en" href="#note-epe-en" aria-label="Note 6">6</a></sup>

REDATA reaches the Senate as the first concrete choice in this story. The bill passed by the Chamber cuts taxes on equipment and requires five commitments: domestic supply, sustainability, clean or renewable power, water efficiency, and research investment. On August 31, Bill 278/2026 was on the September 1 plenary agenda, with Cid Gomes appointed as rapporteur and his report still unpublished.<sup><a id="ref-redata-en" href="#note-redata-en" aria-label="Note 7">7</a></sup>

The bill requires 10% of effective supply to go to the domestic market. Yet it measures compliance through domestic revenue as a share of total revenue. A company may also replace the whole obligation with additional research investment. In the North, Northeast, and Center-West, the commitment falls to 8%. Those rules can attract investment without creating an identifiable block of compute that a Brazilian company, university, or public agency can buy.<sup><a id="ref-chamber-en" href="#note-chamber-en" aria-label="Note 8">8</a></sup>

That distinction organizes both scenarios. Physical capacity describes machines installed in Brazil. Contractable capacity describes services available to Brazilian buyers. Protected continuity describes the share that remains accessible during a crisis or foreign restriction.

### Camila tries to explain the vote

Camila worked as a parliamentary secretary and adviser to a lower-profile member of the Chamber's science and technology committee. On the eve of the vote, she received three drafts of a REDATA memo. The first celebrated investment and jobs. The second warned about water and grid costs. The third asked whether the 10% domestic requirement represented revenue or machines.

The congressman read the last question twice.

“But there is ten percent,” he said.

“Ten percent of revenue,” Camila replied. “If the domestic product costs more, the physical share can be smaller. And the company can replace all of it with R&D.”

“Research also stays in Brazil.”

“It does. It just does not keep a hospital or a bank running during a restriction.”

He asked for a one-page version. He wanted to support the bill without appearing to sign a blank check. Camila called Rafael, a CTIR analyst and her partner, to test the language. He suggested a simpler question: during an emergency, who decides which machines serve Brazil?

Lourdes, Camila's mother and the administrative manager of a low-cost clinic, heard another version of the debate on television. The report showed rows of servers and promised thousands of jobs. Then it interviewed residents concerned about water. Lourdes sent her daughter a message:

> Does this thing help the clinic or just use our electricity?

Camila started an answer about computational capacity. She deleted it and wrote that she did not know yet.

At the office, she put her mother's question at the top of the memo. The answer depended on the law, the contracts, and the government's ability to enforce them. Brazil had one night to debate the first element. The others would take years.

Both scenarios begin here and move through the same external world: a global cyberattack wave in 2027, a second loss-of-control incident that forces a US–China slowdown in 2028, and international compute rationing in 2029. Brazil's response creates the difference. On one path, the country turns energy and territory into contractable capacity, contributes to the agreement, and preserves options. On the other, it commits its assets before negotiating access and accepts a deal designed by those who already control models, chips, and machines.
