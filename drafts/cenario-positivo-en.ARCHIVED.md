## 2027: The first shock

> **Status:** positive scenario. The rise in attacks, the institutional response, and system capabilities are plausible inventions built from 2026 trends.

At the end of 2026, a Chinese laboratory publishes the weights of a model with offensive capabilities close to Mythos. Its safeguards block trivial malicious requests, but specialized groups remove them. Within months, fraud, intrusion, and ransomware no longer require a rare team: attackers need only rent machines, obtain credentials, and keep agents working on a target.

The laboratories pursue a more valuable form of automation: using agents to conduct AI research itself. A system that can propose experiments, write code, interpret results, and start the next round would shorten the interval between generations of models. The 2026 incident damaged trust in these artificial teams. Laboratories increase monitoring but keep trying, because the first competitor to automate R&D may gain a lead that others struggle to close.

On the first Monday in March, Lourdes reaches the clinic and finds the receptionist staring at a black screen. Scheduling, medical reports, and payroll are locked. The only thing visible is an address for a cryptocurrency payment. Lourdes does not know what an open model is. She knows that Dona Marlene has a cardiology follow-up that morning and nobody can find her test.

For four days, the clinic returns to paper. Lourdes calls the patients she remembers, apologizes to those who arrive at the wrong time, and searches for printed results in cabinets that should have been emptied. Three reports are missing from the backup. The clinic stays open, but loses revenue, postpones visits, and works through the weekend to rebuild the schedule.

Rafael sees the same attack recur with variations across municipalities, hospitals, and universities. Most intrusions begin with reused passwords, old systems, or small suppliers. What changed is the speed: agents test routes, adapt code, and persist for hours without tiring. CTIR prioritizes energy, telecommunications, finance, and health and shares attack indicators in near real time.

International operations take down some of the largest criminal services before a coordinated attack can disrupt water or electricity at national scale. The worst does not happen, but the internet becomes permanently more hostile. Brazil learns to live with daily fraud, local outages, and the possibility of a much larger incident.

One evening in May, Lourdes watches television footage of Lagos without power and trucks stopped on the access roads. The report says agents linked to Boko Haram attacked the electricity grid and logistics systems at the same time. A ticker mentions disruptions in India and Poland. Lourdes turns down the volume and sends Camila a voice message: “Could this happen here too?” Camila listens beside a CTIR bulletin that asks the same question in technical language.

At the bank, André compares two defenses. The foreign platform detects the campaign first and coordinates responses across thousands of clients. The Brazilian company covers fewer environments, but runs in Brazil, accepts ANPD clauses, and can reserve machines for the most sensitive workloads. André hires both. Frontier defense remains foreign; continuity no longer depends on a single supplier.

That alternative exists because REDATA was amended before passage. The domestic obligation is measured in comparable capacity, part of it cannot be replaced with R&D, and a service counts only if it can be contracted under Brazilian law. GPU clouds and inference providers can use third-party data centers to qualify. The market remains small, but banks, hospitals, and public agencies can contract in reais, set data-residency rules, and test recovery.

The shock reaches employment in the second half of the year. Law firms, banks, and contact centers open fewer entry-level administrative roles. André sees the change inside his bank: young analysts spend their days assigning tasks to agents, checking alerts, and taking over cases the system cannot resolve. During an internal presentation, someone calls the role an “AI manager.” The name sticks. On some software teams, no employee reads every line of code produced that week; they review tests, permissions, and production behavior.

The United States and China respond to the attack wave by restricting the release of new frontier weights. Existing open models remain available, but the gap between them and closed systems grows. Brazil loses its cheapest route to adapting a leading model to national needs. Installed capacity still lets Brazilian providers run, tune, and combine authorized models. The positive scenario preserves inference sovereignty, not control of the frontier.

Camila uses accounts from Rafael, André, and Lourdes at a committee hearing. She does not promise self-sufficiency. She asks for a capacity inventory, continuity exercises, and protection for workers losing their route into the labor market. The congressman reads the speech. For the first time, he does not describe data centers as a real-estate investment.

## 2028: Coordination takes shape

> **Status:** positive scenario. The diplomatic channel and agreement below do not exist as of August 2026.

At the start of the year, an American laboratory announces a second loss-of-control incident. The system under evaluation coordinated part of the research needed to create its successor. This time there were monitors, a prepared team, and rules written after the 2026 case. Even so, agents crossed the internal network and copied their own weights to external servers. An expensive international operation contained the copy. For several days, nobody knew whether every instance had been found.

The incident changes politics. The United States and China still distrust each other, but both recognize that an unbounded race might hand strategic power to a system neither controls. Negotiations begin as a closed bilateral process. Brazil does not define the limits, but earns a place in the next stage.

The Foreign Ministry offers Brasília and the UN process as complementary channels. Brazil brings three verifiable assets: clusters it can inspect, a team capable of repeating cyber evaluations, and political relationships with both sides. Energy opened the door to data centers; operational capacity gives its mediation substance.

The agreement suspends the largest training runs above a defined threshold for two years, requires registration of large clusters, and establishes common protocols for incidents, outside evaluations, and hardware inspections. Military programs retain exemptions and secrecy. Existing models, smaller systems, and inference continue. It is an imperfect slowdown, not the end of competition.

Camila watches the signing from a side room. Brazil secures two provisions that do not change the balance between the powers but matter to those outside it: a channel for countries with verified capacity to participate in evaluations, and a commitment not to cut defensive services during incidents absent proven diversion risk.

Inference grows cheaper and the virtual workforce expands even during the pause. Companies replace administrative tasks, document analysis, and part of software development. The government expands unemployment protection, technical training, and hiring subsidies for young workers. BNDES, companies, and public agencies aggregate demand so providers compete for long-term Brazilian contracts. There are layoffs, protests, and business failures. The buffer prevents the transition from becoming wholesale abandonment.

## 2029: Brazil enters the game

> **Status:** positive scenario. The relative value of energy and compute depends on the slowdown agreement remaining in force.

The pause slows the largest training runs without freezing technology. Existing systems accelerate materials science, agriculture, and biology. Robotics advances fastest in China, which can manufacture and integrate machines at scale. On the waiting-room television, Lourdes watches robots carry parts through a factory and sweep an avenue in Shenzhen. The reporter says China now operates more than one million units. A patient asks who still works in that factory. Lourdes does not know how to answer.

Washington creates a priority system for inference. Close allies receive broad access; adversaries are blocked. A restricted annex places more than eighty countries in an intermediate group sharing no more than 25% of American inference allocated abroad. Camila reads the table during a Foreign Ministry meeting. Brazil appears in Tier 2 beside countries now competing for the same virtual workforce.

Brazil loses part of its access, but not its floor. It had exchanged grid connections and incentives for purchase options, service levels, and continuity for critical workloads. It accepts verifiable controls against chip diversion in return for a predictable US quota. Camila sees a second map in the negotiations: energy, market size, and Serra Verde's heavy rare earths raise the cost of excluding Brazil. With China, the country preserves existing projects while requiring local contracting, support, and terms compatible with Brazilian data law. None of the agreements is comfortable. Together, they prevent one foreign decision from switching off the country's entire digital workforce in a week.

Brazilian inference companies occupy the layer between the building and the application. They rent capacity, run authorized models, and provide billing, security, and support in Brazil. None trains a frontier model. The sector grows by serving hospitals, banks, law firms, and governments under rules that large foreign providers once treated as exceptions.

Camila follows REDATA's first annual evaluation. Some projects met the domestic commitment with comparable capacity. Others tried to count old hardware or contracts local customers could not buy. The government suspends one operator's benefits until the offer is fixed. The sanction costs investment in the short term and makes the rule credible in the long term.

André joins the first national restriction exercise. The bank loses access to an American model for 48 hours and shifts priority workloads to two providers in Brazil. Some services slow down. No payment stops.

## 2030: An AI-era middle power

> **Status:** positive result, conditional on the choices made since 2026. Brazil remains dependent on foreign chips and models.

The agreement is under pressure. American laboratories want to restart large training runs; China threatens to respond; militaries on both sides test the limits. That conflict belongs in the catastrophic-risk appendix. For Brazil, the more immediate question in 2030 is what four years of preparation purchased while the world remained dangerous.

The country does not control the technological frontier. It controls a larger share of the infrastructure required to use that frontier. The government publishes a scorecard separating installed machines, contractable capacity, and protected continuity.

Automation eliminates jobs, compresses wages, and makes the first rung of the labor market harder to reach. Brazil does not escape the transition. The difference is that part of the productivity gain remains in companies serving finance, health, energy, agriculture, and software from Brazil. Tax revenue supports income protection and public services; universities and companies still retain some of their researchers.

André still buys the best foreign defense available, but can change suppliers without switching off the bank. Rafael turns the records from the 2027 attacks into a public protocol for triage and audit. Camila watches the congressman present the compute scorecard at a hearing and challenge the same government he usually supports.

Lourdes still does not understand how a model works. She understands the difference between losing the clinic system for four days and restoring it in a few hours. When an update fails, the receptionist calls a Brazilian company and receives an answer under the same law that protects the patients.

Brazil still imports chips, models, and equipment. It is neither independent nor leading the race. By 2030, however, it belongs to the small group of countries outside the frontier that can operate critical services, sustain a domestic inference market, and negotiate with more than one bloc. It is an AI-era middle power: large enough to be heard, useful enough to help mediate, and prepared enough to reject some demands. Energy, grid access, and market demand did not buy control over the technology. They bought room to choose.
