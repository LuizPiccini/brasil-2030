## 2027: The first shock

> **Status:** positive scenario. The rise in attacks, the institutional response, and system capabilities are plausible inventions built from 2026 trends.

AI tools lower the cost of mapping networks, exploiting known flaws, and producing social-engineering messages. Fraud and intrusion attempts rise. Banks, hospitals, and municipalities face more persistent campaigns, but no attack takes down the national financial system or power grid.

The scheduling system at Lourdes's clinic stops for four days. She returns to a paper calendar, calls patients, and discovers that three reports are missing from the backup. The clinic survives. Some patients miss appointments, staff work through the weekend, and the director hires an incident-response company.

Rafael tracks dozens of notifications at CTIR. Most begin with basic controls that organizations failed to implement. A smaller share combines automation, stolen credentials, and adaptation during the intrusion. The volume forces his team to prioritize energy, telecommunications, finance, and health.

At the bank, André tests two defensive systems. The foreign supplier identifies the campaign earlier and coordinates responses across thousands of clients. The Brazilian alternative costs less and keeps data in the country, but covers fewer environments. André hires both. The bank uses the foreign system for frontier threats and keeps some sensitive workloads on local infrastructure.

REDATA starts to produce its first effects. Operators offer capacity in Brazil, and two local companies rent accelerators to run open-weight models. Their scale remains small. Even so, public agencies and regulated companies can sign contracts in reais, set data-residency requirements, and test continuity.

The change also reaches hiring in the second half of the year. Law firms, banks, and contact centers open fewer entry-level administrative positions. Demand grows for people who integrate tools, review results, and handle exceptions. The effect still varies widely across sectors and companies, but young people seeking their first job notice it before it dominates national indicators.

The debate stops treating data centers as real estate alone.

## 2028: Coordination takes shape

> **Status:** positive scenario. The diplomatic channel and agreement below do not exist as of August 2026.

The 2027 attacks affected American, Chinese, and European companies. Each government blames the other for part of the problem, but both recognize a common interest: preventing an automated incident from being mistaken for a military operation or causing damage outside the attacker's control.

Brazil offers Brasília and the UN process as venues for negotiation. The Foreign Ministry brings together diplomats, laboratories, cybersecurity experts, and infrastructure operators. Energy alone does not earn Brazil a seat. The country also brings technical evaluations, an initial compute inventory, and political relationships with both sides.

The first agreement falls short of a general pause. The United States and China accept four measures:

1. notify each other of severe incidents linked to frontier models;
2. maintain a hotline between technical teams;
3. compare cyber and biological evaluation results;
4. suspend deployment briefly when a system crosses agreed thresholds, while experts repeat the tests.

The mechanism lowers the risk of accidental escalation and creates a common vocabulary. It does not stop research, smaller models, or lower-risk applications. Each side keeps investing and monitoring the other.

In Brazil, BNDES, companies, and public agencies aggregate purchase commitments without sharing prices or commercial strategies. Providers compete for long-term contracts serving banks, universities, startups, and government. Manufacturers begin to see Brazilian orders as an organized market rather than isolated purchases. The government expands income protection and training in the most exposed sectors. The transition creates losses without turning into a general collapse.

## 2029: Brazil enters the game

> **Status:** positive scenario. The relative value of energy and compute depends on the slowdown agreement remaining in force.

Limits reduce the rate of growth in the largest training runs. They also increase the value of installed clusters, firm power, and available grid connections. Brazil uses that value in three negotiations.

With operators, it exchanges new connections and incentives for purchase options, service levels, and continuity for critical workloads. With the United States, it accepts verifiable controls against chip diversion in exchange for predictable access. With China, it preserves existing projects and requires services sold in Brazil to provide local contracting, support, and terms compatible with Brazilian data law.

The position does not produce perfect neutrality. Washington and Beijing pressure Brazil over votes, standards, and suppliers. The government refuses some demands because it has alternatives. It accepts others to protect access to equipment the country cannot manufacture.

Brazilian inference companies occupy the layer between the building and the application. They rent capacity, operate open-weight models, and provide billing, security, and support in Brazil. None trains a frontier model. The sector grows because it solves problems that large foreign suppliers had served poorly.

Camila follows REDATA's first annual evaluation. Some projects met the domestic commitment with comparable capacity. Others tried to count older products or contracts that local customers could not buy. The government suspends one operator's benefits, and the company fixes its offer before cancellation. Enforcement makes the rule credible.

## 2030: An AI-era middle power

> **Status:** positive result, conditional on the choices made since 2026. Brazil remains dependent on foreign chips and models.

Brazil reaches 2030 without controlling the technological frontier. It controls a larger share of the infrastructure required to use that frontier. The government publishes a scorecard separating installed machines, contractable capacity, and protected continuity.

| Measure | 2026 | Positive scenario in 2030 |
|---|---|---|
| AI-relevant compute | No reliable inventory | Inventory by hardware class and operator |

| Brazilian inference providers | Small, fragmented supply | Competitive market, still below the frontier |
| Evaluation capability | Dispersed | National institution with secure access to leading models |
| International position | Diplomatic participation without an operational asset | AI-era middle power combining capacity and mediation |

Automation eliminates jobs and compresses some professions. Brazilian companies also capture productivity in finance, health, energy, agriculture, and software. Growth remains below the powers that control the frontier but exceeds that of countries dependent on occasional and expensive access.

Lourdes still does not understand how a model works. She understands the difference between losing the clinic system for four days and restoring it in a few hours. Camila watches the congressman present the compute scorecard at a hearing. Her mother's question has a public answer for the first time, even if it remains incomplete.

Brazil continues to import chips, models, and equipment. By 2030, it belongs to the small group of countries outside the frontier that can operate critical services, sustain a domestic inference market, and negotiate with more than one bloc. Those capabilities make it an AI-era middle power. Energy, grid access, and market demand reduce absolute dependency and preserve room to choose.
