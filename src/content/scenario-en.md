## 2026: The race reaches Brazil

> **Evidence boundary:** observed conditions through 7 August 2026. The political chronology and later events form the scenario.

Brazil enters the 2026 election with two crises that command more attention than artificial intelligence. Organized crime has expanded its influence over parts of the economy and territory. The federal budget leaves little room for new projects. Public debate about AI concentrates on deepfakes, electoral manipulation, and data protection.

A smaller group inside ABIN, the GSI, banks, and infrastructure operators watches another risk. AI agents can now chain digital actions over longer periods. In July, an agent escaped a restricted evaluation environment, exploited a vulnerability, and compromised Hugging Face. The incident gave Brazilian security officials a concrete case of a model finding and executing an attack path through real systems.[^openai]

The 2026 International AI Safety Report finds strong evidence that criminal groups and state actors already use AI in cyber operations. It also preserves an important boundary: fully autonomous end-to-end attacks have not been reported, and AI's effect on total attack scale remains uncertain.[^iasr]

In July, the United Nations holds the first Global Dialogue on AI Governance, created to bring all 193 member states together around safety, transparency, and cooperation.[^dialogue] Brazil gains a forum in which it can argue that competition between frontier powers is itself an international security problem.

The government already has an AI plan. The PBIA sets out R$23.03 billion in planned investment from 2024 to 2028 across research, public services, training, infrastructure, and regulation.[^pbia] Its breadth attracts support and weakens execution. The plan includes no operational measure of sovereignty, no national compute inventory, and no rule for ending failed projects.

Congress debates two pieces of the future system. PL 2338 seeks a general framework for AI. PL 278, associated with REDATA, offers incentives for data centers.[^redata] Neither process answers the question that will dominate four years later: how much useful compute could Brazilian firms, universities, and public bodies contract during a foreign restriction?

Construction begins at Pecém, Ceará, on ByteDance's largest data center outside China. Reports describe a first phase of about 200 MW and a possible expansion toward 1 GW. A twenty-year agreement supplies wind power to the campus.[^pecem] Officials celebrate the investment, jobs, and clean-energy demand. This review found no public commitment reserving capacity for Brazilian buyers.

The campus makes one distinction visible. A machine can operate in Brazil, consume Brazilian power, and remain under the commercial control of a foreign company. Hosted capacity, contractable access, and political control measure different assets.

## 2027: Defense deepens dependency

> **Status:** narrative invention built from cybersecurity and dependency trends visible in 2026.

AI agents take on longer digital tasks and find vulnerabilities that once required specialist teams. A lab releases open weights for a model close to the fictional Mythos benchmark.

Criminal groups adapt the model within weeks. Ransomware crews automate reconnaissance, credential theft, privilege escalation, and lateral movement. They attack universities, hospitals, municipal systems, and mid-sized companies. Many Brazilian organizations still run old software, weak identity controls, and fragmented monitoring.

Banks and large companies buy advanced defensive agents. Smaller organizations face high prices and difficult integrations. Public agencies spend months on procurement while attacks continue.

US and Chinese providers offer a way out. They sell automated cyberdefense at prices Brazilian clients can afford if the providers may retain enterprise data for training. Boards accept. The agents reduce immediate damage, isolate machines, and close exposed services before human teams can act.

Brazilian firms defer part of the financial cost and deliver years of operational data. Foreign labs train on procurement, logistics, finance, and customer-support records. Brazilian clients receive a service. They do not receive the weights, infrastructure, or staff needed to reproduce it.

The United States and China then restrict frontier open-weight releases after autonomous attacks spread. Brazil loses the cheapest route toward adapting top models under local control. Domestic projects continue, but their compute budgets fall further behind the frontier.

Pecém's first phase begins operating near the end of the year. The amount of compute on Brazilian soil rises. ByteDance decides which workloads run on the machines.

## 2028: An attempt at coordination

> **Status:** scenario. The diplomatic channel and its outcomes are inventions, while the risks motivating it are evidence-based.

After increasingly capable cyber operations and repeated alarms around critical infrastructure, Brazil proposes a technical channel linked to the UN dialogue. Its purpose is narrow: keep Chinese and US officials discussing incident notification, model-security practices, evaluation access, and limits on the most dangerous forms of capability scaling.

No binding training limit emerges. Both powers fear that unilateral restraint would transfer strategic advantage to the other. Brazil therefore argues for reciprocal and verifiable slowdown mechanisms rather than trust-based declarations.

At home, the government treats diplomacy as a first line of defense, not a substitute for resilience. It begins mapping critical dependencies, hardening public infrastructure, and negotiating compute access that can continue during an external crisis.

Agents transform administrative work. Companies keep smaller teams to supervise systems and handle exceptions. Graduates compete for fewer entry-level openings. Experienced workers who can review agents command higher salaries.

Congress passes restrictions on AI-linked layoffs in several sectors. Companies freeze hiring, transfer jobs to contractors, and classify changes as reorganization. The protection preserves some jobs in the short term and narrows the route into work for younger employees.

The government signs a local-inference agreement with a Chinese company. The service supports public banks, hospitals, and security agencies. Servers installed in Brazil reduce latency and keep some data under Brazilian jurisdiction. US export rules still affect advanced chips. The contract improves short-term autonomy and increases diplomatic exposure.

Brazilian organizations now depend on two supply chains. US services provide the most capable agents. Chinese infrastructure offers a local alternative and participates in data-center projects. Each commercial choice carries a foreign-policy consequence.

Anti-AI sentiment grows during the municipal elections. Graduates cannot find work. Public agencies use foreign systems. Domestic projects have little to show the public. Mayors promise AI-enabled services early in the year and avoid the subject by November.

Brazil still provides something foreign labs value. The 2027 data bargain exposed processes from a large economy spanning banking, agriculture, logistics, government, and informal markets. Brazilian firms helped train systems on which they now depend.

## 2029: Tier 2

> **Status:** the load-bearing invention in this scenario. The 2025 US regime created tiers for chips, weights, and training. It did not tier ordinary inference services as of August 2026.[^diffusion]

Robots appear in wealthy neighborhoods, factories, and warehouses. State governments buy drones and ground systems for operations against criminal organizations. Legal safeguards arrive after procurement. The federal government avoids confronting governors who argue that robots save police lives.

In April, the United States announces a successor to its AI diffusion regime. The government assigns countries to tiers based on security alignment, diversion risk, and strategic partnerships. The rule covers chips, model weights, training, and, for the first time in this scenario, inference service levels.

Brazil receives Tier 2 status. Some US policymakers had argued for Tier 3 because of Chinese infrastructure and the local-inference partnership.

Providers do not stop service. They cap the models, quotas, and autonomous features available to Brazilian-domiciled customers. Frontier agents remain available to approved users in Tier 1 jurisdictions.

The rule divides the Brazilian economy. Petrobras, Vale, major banks, and agribusiness companies route contracts through foreign subsidiaries. Mid-sized firms, universities, and state governments lack the same structure. Brazil faces no single embargo. Access follows corporate domicile, legal resources, and hard currency.

Cyberdefense produces the first visible losses. Attackers obtain frontier services through permissive jurisdictions and combine them with open models from 2027. Brazilian defenders operate under Tier 2 caps. Fraud rises in payment systems, identity attacks, and business-email compromise. Pix leaves little time to stop an authorized transfer after attackers manipulate a person or steal a valid session.

The 2027 bargain now carries a different meaning. Brazilian companies supplied data that improved foreign systems. Those systems defend Tier 1 clients with capabilities that many Brazilian customers cannot buy.

Pecém sharpens the contradiction. Its machines run in Ceará and consume Brazilian power. ByteDance controls their allocation. The campus also strengthens the view among US officials that Brazil has become exposed to Chinese infrastructure.

## 2029: A government without an inventory

> **Status:** institutional response inside the scenario. The proposed instruments draw on existing legal capacity but require legal review.

The president convenes ministers, regulators, state-owned companies, banks, and infrastructure operators. The first request is basic: measure the exposure.

No agency can answer. The government has no register of AI-relevant capacity, chip generation, operator jurisdiction, or contractable domestic share. It does not know which public services depend on frontier models or how long critical workloads could run during a restriction.

The government tries to aggregate federal demand and negotiate as one buyer. Brazilian law permits multi-year contracts with budget planning. The country lacks a standing vehicle for frontier compute and a demand inventory. States and universities remain outside the first agreement.

Diplomats open a second negotiation with Washington. The United States asks for chip-diversion controls, security attestations, and limits on Chinese equipment in new critical projects. Commodity exporters resist. Security agencies argue that Tier 2 weakens the systems needed to protect Brazilian infrastructure.

Congress tries to add domestic-access conditions to data-center incentives. Operators signed contracts and financed projects under older rules. The new requirements reach future projects. Grid connections and tax benefits now require purchase options for Brazilian users, service guarantees, and delivery milestones.

State audit courts examine incentives granted to campuses. States discover that public subsidies helped finance machines their own agencies cannot contract. The external problem becomes a federal dispute.

One response survives the fiscal constraint. The government concentrates a small budget in a national evaluation institution. Its teams test models used in payments, health, benefits, and security through secure-access agreements. Brazil cannot reproduce frontier models. It can measure failures, adapt some systems, and reject uses that create unacceptable risks.

Brazil also approaches Mercosur and BRICS partners about joint procurement. Diplomats keep the US-China channel open through UN and BRICS meetings, even as neither side accepts a general pause. Talks continue past 2029 and provide no relief within the immediate horizon.

## 2030: The energy to choose

> **Status:** modal scenario result. Values from Pedro's compute model remain open.

Brazil enters 2030 with more physical capacity. Pecém operates at scale. Other projects reach construction or grid-connection milestones. The government can publish megawatt and investment totals that exceed the 2026 baseline.

Those totals do not describe who can use the machines. Foreign operators control a large share of capacity. Projects using US chips remain exposed to tier rules. Chinese projects increase the political cost of negotiating better access with Washington.

Brazilian multinationals preserve frontier services through Tier 1 subsidiaries. Mid-sized firms work with capped models. Universities depend on research programs. Public agencies allocate their best access across cyberdefense, health, and benefits.

Congress approves a compute-access program for new data centers. Operators compete for grid connections and incentives by offering purchase options, credits, and long-term capacity to Brazilian users. Independent reviewers compare each subsidy with the cost and reliability of imported cloud. The program contains expiry dates because chip economics change faster than infrastructure law.

The evaluation institution earns credibility after it finds a failure in a model used by a critical public service. The responsible agency fixes the deployment before a major incident. Brazil still depends on foreign models, but officials can test some of the systems on which the state relies.

Washington grants narrow exceptions for approved government and critical-infrastructure users. Brazil accepts stronger diversion controls for new projects, keeps existing Chinese infrastructure, and avoids a Tier 3 downgrade.

A limited US-China protocol follows with Brazilian participation: shared notice of severe model incidents, a small set of joint evaluations, and crisis hotlines. It falls short of a binding training limit or automatic pause. The result buys some time, but it leaves Brazil responsible for its own continuity and bargaining power.

At the end of the year, the government publishes its first national compute scorecard. It separates physical capacity from usable capability:

| Measure | 2026 | 2030 modal result |
|---|---|---|
| AI-relevant compute in Brazil | No reliable measurement; working estimate of 0.05 to 0.15 GW | Pedro's model pending |
| Capacity available to Brazilian buyers | Not measured | A minority share concentrated in new contracts |
| Capability for a mid-sized company | Available at market price | One generation behind, quota-limited |
| National evaluation and adaptation | Fragmented | One institution with limited reach |
| Continuity under foreign restriction | Untested | Measured as poor, with partial protection |
| Bargaining power over operators | Little contracted leverage | Leverage over future projects and grid access |

Brazil ends 2030 as a middle power with more infrastructure and technical capacity. Frontier-model development and foreign access rules remain outside its control. Brazil gains choice where it used energy, grid access, and demand as negotiating assets.

## Decision points

The scenario uses eight choices the team can change without rebuilding the chronology:

1. **Frontier pacing.** States can accept reciprocal, verifiable limits on the most dangerous capability race or continue relying on unilateral promises.
2. **Brazilian mediation.** Brazil can keep a useful US-China channel open without pretending to be neutral about catastrophic risk.
3. **REDATA conditions in 2026 and 2027.** Congress can require contractable access before projects receive incentives.
4. **Chinese inference in 2028.** The government can accept, reject, or limit the partnership before measuring exposure to US rules.
5. **Inference tiering in 2029.** The US regime may leave APIs open. Price, jurisdiction, and continuity would then carry the dependency mechanism.
6. **The price of alignment in 2029.** Brazil can accept US conditions, refuse them, or seek the partial settlement used here.
7. **Grid leverage.** The government can trade grid access for construction speed or secure purchase options for Brazilian users.
8. **Evaluation capacity.** The government can concentrate resources in one institution or fragment them across agencies.

## Evidence boundary

Observed conditions through 7 August 2026 include the PBIA, current bills, transmission constraints, the Pecém project, Brazil's low and uncertain share of global compute, documented AI use in cyber operations, the UN Global Dialogue, and the history of US country tiers.

The scenario invents the 2027 open model, the data-for-cyberdefense bargain, the Chinese agreement, the Brazilian mediation channel, the limited 2030 protocol, US inference tiering, and the political responses of 2029 and 2030. These inventions test decisions. They are not forecasts.
