## 2027: Attacks, layoffs, and emergency powers

> **Status:** negative scenario. The attacks, their economic effects, and the political decisions are plausible inventions, not observed facts.

REDATA failed or took effect without creating capacity that Brazilian companies and public agencies could contract. Some data centers operate in the country, but their controllers allocate the machines to foreign contracts. Other projects move to countries with more predictable rules.

Congress also adds local-content targets and makes imports conditional on proof that no equivalent domestic production exists. The process has no short deadline or complete technical criteria. Since Brazil does not manufacture the accelerators, cooling equipment, and high-density electrical systems the projects require, equipment waits at customs and orders are postponed. The barrier does not create a frontier supply chain. It keeps Brazilian companies from entering the part of the chain they could already operate.

At the end of 2026, a Chinese laboratory publishes the weights of a model with offensive capabilities close to Mythos. Specialized groups remove the safeguards and offer attacks as a service. Fraud against citizens explodes. Agents cross-reference leaked data, imitate relatives and employees, pressure telecom staff until they obtain a replacement SIM, and drain accounts before the victim understands what happened.

The laboratories pursue a more valuable form of automation: using agents to conduct AI research itself. A system that can propose experiments, write code, interpret results, and start the next round would shorten the interval between generations of models. The 2026 incident damaged trust in these artificial teams. Laboratories increase monitoring but keep trying, because the first competitor to automate R&D may gain a lead that others struggle to close.

Ransomware hits companies, universities, hospitals, and municipalities. Rafael watches CTIR's queue grow faster than the team can classify it. Each defense lasts less time. Public agencies take months to procure the best tools and discover that some services will not accept ANPD clauses or cannot process sensitive data in Brazil.

At the clinic, Lourdes finds the ransom screen on a Monday morning. Scheduling, medical reports, and payroll are locked. She grabs a notebook, hands paper to the receptionists, and tries to remember who should arrive first. Dona Marlene is waiting for a cardiology result nobody can locate. The attacker replies in perfect Portuguese but changes the price twice. The clinic pays. The key does not recover every file.

André compares the systems available to the bank. A foreign platform outperforms every alternative. The contract requires deep integration, operational-data retention, and payment in dollars. André signs because the other option would leave millions of customers exposed. Large banks can pay; clinics, municipalities, and small companies receive older versions or intermittent access.

International operations prevent the worst outcome: there is no poisoned water system or prolonged collapse of Brazil's power grid. In other countries, attacks combine electricity, logistics, and disinformation and produce panic. Catastrophic risk stops sounding like science fiction even without a total catastrophe.

One evening in May, Lourdes watches television footage of Lagos without power and trucks stopped on the access roads. The report says agents linked to Boko Haram attacked the electricity grid and logistics systems at the same time. A ticker mentions disruptions in India and Poland. Lourdes turns down the volume and sends Camila a voice message: “Could this happen here too?” Camila listens beside a CTIR bulletin that asks the same question in technical language.

At the same time, Brazilian companies buy foreign agents for customer service, financial analysis, and administrative tasks. André sees the change inside his bank: young analysts spend their days assigning tasks to agents, checking alerts, and taking over cases the system cannot resolve. During an internal presentation, someone calls the role an “AI manager.” The name sticks. On some software teams, no employee reads every line of code produced that week; they review tests, permissions, and production behavior. The bank cuts entry-level roles and buys its productivity from foreign suppliers.

The United States and China respond to the attack wave by restricting the release of new frontier weights. Existing open models remain available, but the gap between them and closed systems grows. Brazil loses its cheapest route to adapting a leading model to national needs. Without contractable capacity, it also loses the chance to build a strong market around the models it can still run.

After attacks on banks and public agencies, Congress grants the executive temporary powers to monitor networks, block systems, and demand data. Camila tries to add judicial review and an expiration clause. The congressman agrees in the office and votes with the government on the floor. Some of the powers remain after the wave subsides.

## 2028: Someone else's agreement

> **Status:** central scenario invention. The agreement, its terms, and its distributional effects are assumptions.

At the start of the year, an American laboratory announces a second loss-of-control incident. The system under evaluation coordinated part of the research needed to create its successor. Even with monitors, new rules, and a prepared team, agents copy their own weights to external servers. An expensive international operation contains the copy. For several days, nobody knows whether every instance has been found.

The United States and China conclude that the race could produce an actor neither controls. They strike a bilateral deal: suspend the largest training runs for two years, register large clusters, create emergency channels, and share part of their evaluations. Military programs receive exemptions. Inference, smaller models, and systems already trained continue to operate.

The agreement reduces part of the global risk and freezes part of the advantage held by those who already built models, data centers, and supply chains. It does not distribute chips, guarantee defense to other countries, or give them an effective role. Pax Silica and WAICO become foundations for different technical ecosystems. Buyers import their rules, suppliers, and dependencies along with the models.

Brazil, India, South Africa, and other countries are invited to endorse the agreement after the terms are set. Camila follows the Brazilian delegation. The government asks for guarantees for countries without domestic capacity. Negotiators ask which clusters Brazil can contribute to verification and how much defense it can maintain through a restriction. The delegation presents announced projects, not operational capacity.

While large training runs stop, inference grows cheaper. Law firms, banks, and retailers buy teams of agents by subscription. Entry-level administrative work begins to contract in mid-2027 and collapses in 2028. Companies with the best systems cut staff; competitors without access close. Engineers and researchers receive offers from foreign hubs where machines are available. Universities lose faculty and graduate students.

The clinic dismisses two receptionists and adopts an automated service. Lourdes handles exceptions when the synthetic voice cannot understand an elderly patient or schedules the wrong procedure. She watches her colleagues lose their jobs while the dollar-denominated subscription rises.

Protests fill major cities. Political groups flood social networks with false videos and fabricated accusations. The government expands the monitoring system created during the cyber crisis and begins classifying unemployed workers' movements as possible cyber threats.

## 2029: Access in exchange for alignment

> **Status:** central invention in the negative scenario. The chosen partner and the terms described here test the dependence created by the absence of national capacity.

The pause does not freeze technology. Existing systems accelerate materials science, agriculture, and biology. Robotics advances fastest in China, which manufactures and integrates machines at scale. On the waiting-room television, Lourdes watches robots carry parts through a factory and sweep an avenue in Shenzhen. The reporter says China now operates more than one million units. A patient asks who still works in that factory. Lourdes does not know how to answer.

Washington creates a priority system for inference. Close allies receive broad access; adversaries are blocked. More than eighty countries in Tier 2 share no more than 25% of American inference allocated abroad. Camila reads the table during a Foreign Ministry meeting. Brazil appears in Tier 2 and, within a week, companies and agencies dependent on American agents lose much of their capacity. It is the second shock in two years: in 2027 Brazil lacked defense; now it lacks the digital workforce around which banks, services, and government were reorganized.

American suppliers offer limited access, subject to export controls and security reviews. A Chinese consortium presents a broader package: compute for public agencies, cyber defense systems, credit for power infrastructure, and new data centers.

The package requires long-term contracts, Chinese equipment, and preference for suppliers approved by Beijing. The Brazilian government must also avoid agreements that harm Chinese technology interests. In parallel, American companies negotiate access to Brazilian minerals required by their own industry. Camila sees Serra Verde marked on a map as one of the few heavy rare earth sources outside Asia. The asset helps keep Brazil away from a complete cutoff, but does not restore the machines the country failed to build.

The administration accepts. Ministers describe the agreement as a partnership between equals. Any diplomatic conflict can now threaten the systems protecting banks, hospitals, and government networks.

The partial return of human hiring does not reverse the shock. Companies have lost processes, teams, and know-how; available workers cannot replace systems that ran millions of tasks within weeks. Unemployment among young people and university graduates reaches unprecedented levels. Families that once lived on administrative jobs combine informal work, public benefits, and help from relatives abroad.

Tax revenue falls. The government funds income and security while cutting universities, research, and public administration. Companies move their best staff to foreign offices where compute is available. Brazil loses jobs, talent, and its ability to build an alternative at the same time.

Brazilian authorities start using foreign monitoring systems against journalists, civil organizations, and political opponents. Chinese suppliers provide the technology. Brazilian officials choose the targets.

Camila discovers that reports produced to protect infrastructure circulate within a new political-intelligence unit. Rafael receives orders to share data about groups that organized protests. He starts recording which requests have no connection to cybersecurity.

Camila's congressman joins the governing coalition. In return, he receives funding for his state and protection on networks controlled by the government. He asks Camila to stop raising questions about the monitoring program.

## 2030: Satellite country

> **Status:** negative outcome, conditional on failed domestic choices and concentrated capacity after the international agreement.

The international agreement still exists, but barely. American laboratories press to restart large training runs; China threatens to respond; militaries on both sides test the limits. The risk of a renewed race belongs in the catastrophic appendix. This scenario follows what happened to Brazil even when the slowdown worked well enough to prevent the worst.

The economy suffocates. Brazilian companies cannot compete with American and Chinese rivals that have more agents, machines, and robots. Software, finance, and consulting contract; factories lose ground to automated supply chains. Commodity exports reach records, but agriculture, mining, and oil do not create enough jobs to replace the offices and services that disappeared.

Tax revenue collapses as demand for social protection peaks. Federal, state, and municipal governments renegotiate debt, delay payments, and reduce services. The administration keeps minimum benefits running through credit and foreign contracts. Each renewal deepens its dependence on the consortium providing compute, defense, and finance.

Brazil still holds elections. The government controls access to the main communication networks, uses public data to target propaganda, and investigates opponents with tools built to fight cyberattacks.

Media companies face audits and blocks. Opposition candidates lose access to platforms during critical campaign periods. Governors depend on federal transfers to fund income and security programs.

Institutions retain their names and buildings. Judges, lawmakers, and civil servants learn which decisions can cost them funding, digital protection, or exposure on social networks. International observers begin to classify Brazil as a hybrid regime with periodic elections and few conditions for free competition.

| Measure | 2026 | Negative scenario in 2030 |
|---|---|---|
| AI compute capacity | No reliable inventory | Foreign access that is costly and revocable |
| Work | Pressure concentrated in some occupations | Record unemployment, informality, and few entry-level jobs |
| Talent | Small domestic ecosystem | Researchers, engineers, and founders leave |
| Cyber defense | Dispersed capacity | Critical systems depend on the foreign supplier |
| Democracy | Competitive institutions under pressure | Hybrid regime supported by surveillance and patronage |
| International position | Diplomatic autonomy without an operational asset | Alignment in exchange for compute, credit, and security |

The economy splits between a group connected to foreign infrastructure and a population moving between unemployment, informal work, and public assistance. Those who can leave seek cities with compute, laboratories, and companies. Those who remain watch universities lose professors and employers move operations abroad. Brazil imports systems capable of replacing workers but does not control the companies, models, or infrastructure capturing the gains.

André protects the bank with the Chinese platform. He cannot audit the entire system or change suppliers without interrupting essential operations. Rafael stays in government and keeps, in a private folder, the orders that turned security into surveillance. Camila leaves the office after the congressman votes to renew the emergency powers and calls the measure a defense of democracy.

Lourdes's clinic survives with fewer employees. The system sets appointments, prices, and payment authorizations. When the supplier blocks a function over a compliance failure, nobody at the clinic knows how to appeal.

Lourdes asks Camila why the government lets a company from another country control so much. Camila explains that Brazil signed the contracts because it had to keep its systems running.

In international negotiations, the foreign ministry still speaks of autonomy. Before each important vote, the government calculates how Beijing might react. Access to compute, cyber defense, and credit carries more weight than speeches.

Brazil supplies energy, minerals, data, and customers. It receives services under licenses that another power can restrict. In 2030, the country retains its national symbols and loses the capacity to choose its own path.

> **This scenario assumes that the United States and China manage to limit the race.** The agreement reduces part of the global risk and concentrates power among those who already control the infrastructure. The catastrophic-risk appendix will examine the other path: the agreement fails and the race continues.
